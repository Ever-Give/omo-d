Chapter 1 — Brynhollow

The bell for matins did not ring in Brynhollow. There was no one left in the city who would have rung it. So Iya woke on her own, the way she had woken every morning for the last five years, a quarter-hour before dawn with her eyes already open and her breath already even, as though some discipline deeper than thought was running her body now and would keep running it whether she consented or not.

She lay still for a count of ten. The straw in the mattress shifted under her shoulder. Above her, the rafters of the chandler’s attic creaked in the wind off the river, a wet, fish-belly wind that crawled in under the eaves and laid itself along her arm like a second skin. Somewhere down in the street a procession was already moving. She could hear the bells of it, small and dull, and the wooden clatter of the boards the debt-bearers wore around their necks. A man behind on his tithes, perhaps. A woman who had borrowed against a child. The bells were tin and the procession was small, which meant the debt was small, which meant the man or woman would only be flogged at the end of it and not hanged. Brynhollow’s mercies were in its arithmetic.

She sat up. She folded the rough blanket into a square and set it at the foot of the cot. She crossed the three paces to the washstand and broke the skin of ice on the basin with her knuckle. She washed her face, and dried it on the same coarse cloth she had dried it on yesterday, and the day before. Her pack stood already shouldered against the wall, straps coiled, buckles aligned. Her boots stood beside it, toes to the wall, laces tucked inside the cuffs. The papers that named her Yssa of Coldmere, scribe-for-hire, third daughter of nobody, sat folded in the inner seam of her coat where her thumb could find them in the dark.

There was nothing in the room out of place because she did not yet trust the room. A room could be searched. A room could be entered while one slept. A woman who left her boots scattered in a strange city was a woman who had begun to believe she was safe, and Iya had been instructed at length and at cost in the matter of believing oneself safe.

She knelt at the foot of the cot, on the bare boards, and she set her palms together, and she said the morning devotion under her breath.

Lantern that does not gutter. Light that does not ask. Carry me through the dark places. Carry me back from them.

She said it the way she had been taught to say it — without fervor, without performance, the words as plain as bread. The Lantern did not require fervor. The Lantern required only that you keep walking. She finished, and she opened her eyes, and for a moment she simply knelt there in the gray light and listened to a city that did not know her god and would not have welcomed her if it had.

A child was crying somewhere two streets over. Had been crying, she realized, the whole time she had been awake. No adult voice answered it. After a while it stopped, not because it had been comforted but because crying took breath and breath ran out.

Iya rose. She unbuckled her pack and took out the trial-token, the small disk of dark iron the seer had pressed into her palm in the chapter house at Veld, a lifetime and three hundred miles ago. She held it in her closed hand for the count of one slow breath. She did not look at it. Looking at it was a kind of asking, and she had been told not to ask. She had been told to do exactly what was needed, become who was needed.

She put it away and buckled the pack. She took up her boots and sat on the edge of the cot to lace them, one eyelet at a time, and she thought, as she did every morning now, that the worst part of the work so far was not the danger or the lying or the loneliness. The worst part was how easily she had stepped out of the woman she had been, and how the new woman fit.

By the time Iya stepped into the street, Brynhollow had begun the long, grudging business of waking up.

The chandler’s shop sat on the lower edge of the Middle Wards, where the city’s stone began to remember it had once been built well and the timber-and-wattle of the Low Wards crowded up against it like a tide that had not yet been told to stop. Iya pulled the door shut behind her and stood for a moment under the eaves to set the day in her head. The wind off the river had not improved. It carried the tannery smoke up from the southern flats and laid it over the streets in a thin brown veil that caught in the throat. She drew her scarf up over her nose.

Brynhollow climbed. That was the first thing a stranger learned about it. The city had been built into the side of a long hill that fell away to the river in a series of terraces, and over the centuries the terraces had hardened into law: the High Quarter at the crown, pale-stoned and clean and walled, where the council met and the merchant houses kept their daughters; the Middle Wards spilling down through three cramped tiers of guildhalls and counting-houses and modest temples to gods nobody loved very much; and at the bottom, sprawling along the riverbank in a black smear of rotted wood and standing water, the Low Wards. The Low Wards did not have names. They had directions. Down by the eel docks. Down past the old gate. Down where the Wash comes out. Iya had been in Brynhollow for thirteen days now and she had learned to read the city by which way a person flinched when she asked for directions.

She walked east, toward the market at Penny Cross, with her satchel under her arm and her head down. She was a small woman — slight through the shoulders, narrow in the wrist, the kind of build that the dormitory matrons had always called useful for fitting in places and that her own honest assessment called easy to break. She kept her hair cropped to the jaw, plain brown, nothing about it to remember. Her skin was pale in the way of people from the cold north, which in Brynhollow marked her as foreign without marking her as anything in particular; the city saw enough northerners passing through on the river trade that one more drew no second look. She had been counting on that. So far the city had obliged.

Penny Cross was already loud. The fishmongers had been at their stalls since before she had risen, and the bread-women had set out their boards, and a thin woman with no teeth was crying “pins, pins, good pins” in a voice that suggested she had been crying it for thirty years and had nothing else left in her throat. Iya took up her usual place at the corner of a covered stall where the cobbler let her sit for a copper a day, and she opened her writing-board across her knees, and she waited.

The work came, as it always did. It came in the form of a widow who needed a writ of claim copied for the magistrate, and a carter who needed a bill of lading made out for a load of salt, and a young man with raw red hands who needed a letter written to a girl in another city explaining, haltingly, that he had not forgotten her. Iya wrote each one in her even, schooled hand, and she charged the prices the cobbler had told her to charge, and she listened.

That was the trick of it. A scribe-for-hire was a piece of furniture. People talked over a scribe the way they talked over a dog. By the third day in Penny Cross she had learned which baker was sleeping with which baker’s wife, and which constable took bribes and which only pretended to, and which streets the watch did not walk after the eighth bell. By the seventh day she had learned the names of the council and the names of their mistresses. She had not yet learned anything that smelled like a demon. Thirteen days in, she had begun to wonder whether she was looking in the wrong end of the city.

The salt-carter was still talking when the rumor first reached her.

“—and Vossic dead in his own study, three nights gone, did you hear?”

The bread-woman at the next stall, a broad person with arms like hams and a permanent scowl, snorted. “Heard it from my sister’s boy. Heard the door was bolted from the inside.”

“Bolted and barred. Throat open from here to here.” The carter drew a finger across his own neck, casual as a man describing a fish. “Watch is calling it self-murder.”

“Watch’ll call anything anything if a councilman’s wife slips them a purse.”

“Aye, well. Wife or no wife, a man doesn’t cut his own throat sitting at his own desk with his own pen still in his hand. My cousin’s a clerk in the High Hall, he says the ink wasn’t even dry on the page Vossic was writing.”

Iya’s pen did not stop moving. She finished the line of the bill of lading. She blotted it. She did not look up.

“What was he writing?” the bread-woman said.

The carter shrugged. “Letter to someone. Cousin didn’t see who to. Said the page got taken by the watch before anyone could read it proper.”

“Taken by the watch and burned by the watch, more like.”

“Aye. Likely.”

They moved on, to other deaths and other small corruptions, and Iya wrote out the carter’s bill and took his three coppers and inclined her head and watched him go. She set the writing-board flat across her knees. She breathed out, once, slowly, through her nose.

A councilman dead behind a bolted door. A letter half-written. A watch already lying about it.

It was not proof of anything. It was not even a thread. It was a snag in the weave of the city, the kind of snag that a careful eye might catch and a hurried eye might miss, and Iya had spent thirteen days learning to keep a careful eye. She had been reaching into smoke since she had arrived in Brynhollow. This was the first thing her hand had closed on that felt like it might be rope.

She bent her head over the writing-board and took up the next customer, a girl with a scraped face who needed a letter to her brother in the levies, and she wrote the letter slowly and well, and underneath the writing her mind had already begun to walk uphill toward the High Quarter and the dead man’s house.

She left Penny Cross at the turn of the noon bell, with seventeen coppers in her purse and the taste of tannery smoke still at the back of her throat. The cobbler waved her off without looking up.

She took the long way back toward the chandler’s. Not because she needed to, but because the long way ran past the shrine, and a woman who broke her own small habits was a woman who would break larger ones soon enough.

The shrine stood at the corner where Cooper’s Lane met the broader run of Iron Street, a niche set into the wall of a counting-house, no deeper than her forearm, with an iron grille across its mouth. Inside the niche, in better days, there had been a small clay lamp and a basin for the wax and a folded scrap of prayer-cloth in the order’s pale cream. Iya knew it had been a Lantern shrine because she had been told so before she left Veld. Every city of any size on the eastern road had once had at least one. The order kept lists.

The lamp was still there. The basin was still there. The wick had been pulled out and stolen — for kindling, most likely, since wicks could be sold by the inch in the Low Wards in winter — and the wax had been scraped from the basin down to the clay. The prayer-cloth was gone, and someone had pissed against the grille recently enough that the smell of it still rose in the noon warmth. A child’s chalk drawing covered most of the niche’s back wall: a stick figure with a sun for a head, or possibly a flower. It was hard to tell.

She did not stop. Stopping at a Lantern shrine in Brynhollow would have marked her as surely as kneeling in the street. She walked past it at her usual pace, with her eyes on the cobbles ahead, and she did not turn her head. She felt a small mean flare of anger in her chest that the city had let this happen, and a larger, quieter sorrow underneath it that the city had not even bothered to be cruel about it. The shrine had not been desecrated. It had been forgotten, and then used, the way an empty doorway gets used.

The order’s reach was thin this far east. She had known that going in. The Lantern was a small faith and an old one, and it had never traded well in cities; cities preferred gods who promised things, and the Lantern promised only that you would not have to walk in the dark alone, which was a thin promise to a man who had never been afraid of the dark. Out here she was one of perhaps a dozen sworn sisters within a week’s ride. There would be no rescue if she failed, even Aldis had told her such. The order did not have the bodies to spare.

She turned down an alley that stank of wet straw and came out two streets closer to home, and as she walked she let herself think, for the first time since waking, about the seer.

Mother Veyle had taken her into the small chapel at Veld a fortnight before Iya had ridden out, and they had knelt together in front of the altar lamp, and the seer, a woman so old her skin had gone the color of tallow and her eyes had gone the color of nothing, had pressed the iron token into Iya’s palm and closed Iya’s fingers over it one by one, with her own dry papery hand, as if Iya were a child being given a coin for the market.

I have seen you, the seer had said. A girl with a lantern, going down into a dark place. I do not know what is at the bottom of it. The seer had not seemed to hear her.

You will sin, she had said, in the same flat voice. You must. The work cannot be done without sin, and the work must be done. Every sin will be lifted from you when you return. I do not say this to comfort you. I say this so that you do not hesitate at the moment when hesitation will kill you.

If you return with the thing’s heart in your hand, you will be a sainted sister of this order. If you do not return, you will not be mourned. Your name will be struck from the rolls. No prayer will be said for you. No lamp will be lit. Do you hear me, child.

I hear you, Mother.

Good. Then go and be afraid, and then go and do it anyway.

Iya had carried the words three hundred miles east on a barge and a cart and her own two feet, and she had set them down beside her cot in the chandler’s attic like a stone she could not put further from herself.

What troubled her was not the fear. The fear was clean; the fear she could pray through. What troubled her was the door the seer’s words had opened. Every sin. Every one. A door she had spent five years in the dormitories learning to keep shut — bolt oiled, hinges checked, the frame inspected for rot. And a voice on the other side of it, her own voice, that had begun, faintly, to ask what was on the far side.

She had not opened the door. She would not open it. She had been chosen for this work because she could be trusted not to open it even when she was told it had been unlocked for her.

The Vossic estate stood near the top of the High Quarter, on a street of pale houses where the gutters ran clean because someone was paid to clean them. There was nothing to catch on. Her hair was dark and pinned close. Her face was handsome in the still, careful way of a face that has been taught not to give itself away. A single attendant followed her down the steps, a woman in plain servant’s grey who carried nothing and did nothing and seemed to exist as a kind of grammatical mark beside her mistress.

Mirelle Avon turned left at the bottom of the steps and began to walk up the street, unhurried, and Iya felt it.

She had been told, in the dormitories, what the corruption-sense would feel like in the world. A wrongness in the shape of the soul, the instruction had said. The kind of phrase the order used when it had no better phrase, and which Iya had memorized and not understood. In training they had given her relics to feel for. A knife that had killed three children, a coin that had bought a betrayal, a small wooden idol that had been carved with intent toward a god the order would not name. She had felt those, faintly, the way one feels a draft from a closed door. She had passed her sensings with marks high enough to be sent on her trial.

What she felt now, sitting on her stool across the street with her board on her knees, was not a draft.

It was a smell. It was a taste at the back of her teeth that she couldn’t shake. It was the sensation of having put her bare hand, in the dark, on a thing that was warm and should not have been warm, the small recoil of the body before the mind has caught up. Her stomach turned over once, hard, and the half-finished letter on her board doubled in her vision, and she had to set the heel of her hand flat against the stone of the doorstep to keep herself upright.

The demon is real, she thought.

It was not the first thought she would have wished to have. She would have wished, perhaps, for thank the Lantern, or for I am ready, or for some other proper phrase out of the books. What she had instead was a small bright flare of satisfaction in her chest, the satisfaction of a hunter whose snare has finally moved, and she was alarmed by how warm it felt and how readily it came.

She made herself breathe. She made herself look down at the board, at the meaningless letter, at the ink drying in the pen. When she looked up again Mirelle Avon was at the top of the street, turning the corner toward the High Quarter’s market square, her grey attendant a half-step behind her. The corruption-sense thinned as she went, the way a smell thins when its source moves off. By the time the woman was out of sight it was only a faint sourness at the back of Iya’s tongue.

Iya sat where she was for another quarter of an hour. She did not trust her legs. When she did stand, she gathered her board and her papers slowly and deliberately, and she walked back toward the chandler’s by a route that did not pass anywhere near the Avon townhouse, and she did not let herself think, on the way, about the warmth of the satisfaction, or about how much like hunger it had felt.

She would meet Aldis tomorrow. There was a wash-house in the Low Wards where one left a folded scrap of cloth in a particular window when one needed to be seen, and she would leave the cloth tonight, on the way to fetch her supper, and Aldis would come.

She had a name now. She had a face. She had the first true smear of the thing on a living person, and she had not flinched from it, and she would not flinch.

That was what she told herself, walking home through the noon smoke with her hands quiet at her sides and the taste of the corruption-sense still faint at the back of her teeth.

That she would not flinch.

Chapter 2 — The Leaky Tap

The wash-house stood at the bottom of a lane that smelled of lye and river-rot, in a quarter of the Low Wards where the buildings leaned together overhead until the strip of sky between them was no wider than a man’s hand. Iya came to it after dark, by the route Aldis had taught her. Three turnings she did not need, two doublings-back, a long slow loop past a butcher’s stall whose dog she had been feeding scraps for a week now and who knew her step. The dog did not bark. That was the test of the route. If the dog had barked she would have walked past the wash-house and gone home.

She left her folded scrap of cloth in the window of the front room. She went round to the back. The door was unbolted, as it always was when Aldis was inside, and Iya let herself in and let the bolt fall behind her.

Aldis was at the long stone trough in the back room, sleeves rolled, forearms in to the elbow in graying water. She had a sheet wadded against the washboard and she was working it with the slow, methodical force of a woman who had been doing this all evening and would do it for another hour after Iya left, because the cover was the cover and the cover did not pause for sentiment. She did not look up when Iya came in. She finished the line of the sheet she was working. Then she set it aside and dried her hands on her apron and turned, and her face, which had been a laundress’s face, closed, tired, indifferent, now softened into something Iya had not seen on it in two weeks.

“Child,” she said.

Iya crossed the small room and let Aldis take her face between two damp hands. Aldis was a broad woman, broader than Iya by half, with iron-gray hair pulled back severely and the lined, weathered skin of someone who had spent her thirties walking in weather. Her hands smelled of lye and faintly, underneath the lye, of the tallow soap she had carried with her from Veld and rationed out a thumbnail at a time so that she would not have to use the harsh river soap that the laundresses here used, which would have cracked her hands open in a week.

“You’re thinner,” Aldis said.

“I’m fine.”

“You’re thinner. Are you eating?”

“I’m eating, Mother. I bought an eel pie at Penny Cross today and ate the whole of it.”

“Speak,” Aldis said.

“Say it back to me. I want to hear it from you tonight.”

Iya breathed in. The steam in the room was very warm. “You can guide. You can witness. You can intervene if my life is in immediate danger and not before. You cannot lie for me. You cannot steal for me. You cannot lay a hand in violence for me. You cannot…” Her voice caught a little, she did not know why; she made it go on. “You cannot sin for me. Every vow you took at your elevation binds you still. The trial is mine. The sins are mine. The task is mine.”

“And if I’m made to sin for you?”

“Then the trial is void. And both of us are damned.”

“Yes. Both of us.” Aldis’s hand tightened on hers, just once. “I want you to understand that, child. Not as a rule. As a thing that is true. If I see you about to do a thing that breaks you, and I step in front of it because I love you, I do not save you. I lose you, and I lose myself, and the thing in this city goes on. The kindness I am permitted to give you is to watch, and to pray, and to be here in this room when you come back. That is all. Do you understand me?”

“I understand you.”

“Look at me when you say it.”

Iya looked at her. Aldis’s eyes were very steady. There was no wetness in them and there would not be; Aldis was not a woman who wept where it could be seen, and probably not where it could not.

“I understand you, Mother.”

“Good.” Aldis released her hand. She did not look away. “Then I will tell you the other part, which they do not put in the books because they do not trust the young to hear it. The freedom you have been given is real. Every sin you take into yourself for this work will be lifted from you when you come home. Not weighed. Not measured against a ledger. Lifted. As if it had never been. That is the gift of the trial and it is a true gift, and I will not pretend to you that it is not also a terrible one.”

“Why terrible?”

“Because you will find out what you are willing to do. A woman who has not been given that freedom does not know what she is, only what she has been kept from being. You will know. Whatever you do down there, in whatever dark you find — you will know, after, that you were a person who could do it. The sins go with your elevation. The knowledge stays.”

Iya was quiet for a long time.

“Did you know, Mother? After yours.”

“I knew.”

“And did the knowing go away?”

Aldis smiled, very small, very tired. “No, child. The knowing did not go away. But I learned to carry it. And I learned that the carrying was not a punishment. It was the thing that made me of any use to a girl like you, sitting on a bench in a wash-house at the wrong end of a city, three hundred miles from anyone who loves her.” She reached out again and touched Iya’s cheek, briefly, with the back of two fingers. “Now go. And eat more. You’re thinner.”

Iya stood. Her legs felt slightly unsteady, the way legs felt after standing up from a long warm bath. She gathered her cloak from where she had laid it over the bench.

“Mother?”

“Yes.”

“Pray for me.”

“I have not stopped, child. Go.”

Iya unbolted the back door and stepped out into the cold lye-stinking lane, and she pulled the door to behind her, and she stood for a moment with her hand on the wood and her eyes closed. Inside, she heard the small wet slap of a sheet being lifted again to the washboard. Aldis would work for another hour. Iya pulled her hood up and started the long walk back.

Two nights later, Iya was on a roof of a shop opposite the Avon townhouse with her cloak pulled close and her breath coming in small white plumes, watching the black door under its brass knocker.

She had been there since the eighth bell. She had brought a folded square of oilcloth to sit on and a heel of bread to eat, and she had taken her water in small careful sips because she did not yet know how long the night would be.

Just past the tenth bell, the black door opened. Mirelle Avon came down the steps alone.

No attendant tonight. No carriage. A plain dark cloak over the gray dress, the hood up, the gloves still to the elbow. She paused at the bottom of the steps and stood for a moment with one hand on the railing, looking up the street, as if listening for something. Then she turned left and began to walk.

Iya was off the roof and into the street within the count of twenty.

She kept her distance. Mirelle did not look back, not once, but Iya kept her distance anyway because a woman who never looked back was either stupid or accustomed to being followed and unbothered by it, and Mirelle was not stupid. Iya followed at a half-street’s remove, on the opposite side, with her hood low and her step soft on the cobbles.

Mirelle walked slowly. Carefully. But there was something else in it tonight, something Iya could not at first name. The woman’s gait had a held quality. As if each step were being placed deliberately rather than allowed to fall. Twice in the first three streets Mirelle stopped, once at a fountain in a small square, where she set her gloved hand against the lip of the basin and stood with her head bent for the count of perhaps ten breaths before going on, and once at the corner of a glover’s shop, where she simply stood for a moment with her eyes closed, as Iya watched from the mouth of an alley and could not tell what she was doing.

She was not sick. She was not drunk. Iya knew the walks of women who were sick or drunk and this was neither of those. There was something deliberate in it, something she was attending to, the way a woman might attend to a small fragile thing she was carrying in cupped hands.

The route made no sense for a highborn woman alone at night either. Mirelle came down out of the High Quarter by a side street, crossed through the Middle Wards by a route that took her past three guildhalls and the back of a tannery, and then, without any change in pace, without any apparent hesitation, turned down into the Low Wards.

A woman like Mirelle Avon, alone, in a dark cloak, in the Low Wards, after the tenth bell. It should have ended her in the first lane. It did not. Mirelle walked past it all without concern.

The corruption-sense was thickening on the air. Not just on Mirelle now — around her, a faint sour smear that grew denser as they moved deeper, the way a smell grows denser as one walks toward its source. Iya’s stomach had begun to do the slow turning-over again. She made herself breathe through her mouth.

Mirelle turned down a narrow lane Iya had not been in before. It was close and dark and smelled of beer and old straw and something older, something underneath, and at the end of it a single lantern burned over a tavern door. Iya stopped at the mouth of the lane and pressed herself against the wall.

The sign over the door was worn nearly to nothing. Iya could not read it from where she stood, but she did not need to read it. She could see the bouncer.

He was an enormous man, the size and shape of a smithy door, in a leather jerkin that strained at the shoulders. He stood under the lintel with his arms folded and his face composed into the particular blankness of men who were paid to be blank. Mirelle came up the lane toward him and he did not speak and she did not speak, and he stepped sideways without unfolding his arms, and the door opened for her without anyone touching it that Iya could see, and she went in, and the door closed.

The corruption-sense overwhelmed her.

She had to put her hand against the wall. She bent her head and breathed through her mouth and the smell that was distinctly demonic sat thick at the back of her teeth and behind her eyes and in the hollow under her tongue, and for a long moment she could not tell whether she was about to be sick or only to weep, and she did neither.

When she looked up again, she could read the sign. The lamp over the door cast just enough light. Letters cut into wood and weathered down to ghosts of themselves: The Leaky Tap.

She stood at the mouth of the lane for another minute, perhaps two. She did not approach the door. To approach the door tonight would be to die at the door tonight, and Aldis had not taught her to rush. She marked the lane in her head. The turn off the main run, the angle of the buildings, the placement of the lamp. She turned and walked back the way she had come, slowly, with her hood low, with the corruption-sense thinning behind her at every step.

By the time she reached the chandler’s it was past midnight. She climbed the narrow stair without lighting a candle. She let herself into the attic and slid the bolt and stood for a moment in the dark with her back against the door.

The thing is in there, she thought. Or under there. Or it has roots there.

She did not know which. She did not need to know yet. She knew the door now, and she knew there was a man at the door, and she knew the kind of woman who was let through it without speaking, and that was three things she had not known this morning.

She washed her face in cold water and lay down on the cot and did not sleep for a long time.

She gave herself the next morning to think, and then she went to work.

The garret above the chandler’s shop had served for her intel gathering but it was the wrong garret for the Tap. Iya spent the better part of a day walking the lanes around the tavern in widening circles, with her writing-board under her arm and the air of a scribe who had got herself lost looking for an address, until she found what she needed: a third-floor room above a cobbler’s-and-tallow shop two streets over, with a single shutter that opened onto a sightline down the length of the lane to the Tap’s door. The cobbler asked no questions and took her week’s rent in advance and gave her a key that did not fit the lock properly. She did not complain about the key. A door that did not lock properly from the outside also did not lock properly from the inside, and a woman keeping watch on a tavern at the bottom of a city had reason to prefer a door she could leave through quickly.

She moved her watch in that night. She brought a stool, a folded blanket, the heel of bread, and the small clay pot she had been pissing in. Everything she’d need for her stakeout. The cover story was a scribe-for-hire taking lodgings while she copied a long manuscript for a client; if anyone asked, she had the half-finished pages of a real manuscript on the table to show them. They did not ask. The cobbler’s tenants did not speak to one another. That was the whole of the appeal of a cobbler’s-and-tallow shop in this part of the Low Wards.

She watched the Tap for four nights.

The pattern became clear quickly. The Tap did not open to the public in any sense that Iya recognized. Its door admitted perhaps thirty people across the course of an evening — never more, sometimes fewer — and the bouncer turned away anyone who did not belong, with the same sideways unfolding-of-arms gesture he had used to admit Mirelle but reversed in meaning. He did not speak to those he turned away. He simply did not move, and after a moment they understood and went elsewhere. Twice in the four nights she watched a small drunk knot of sailors come up the lane and try the door; both times they left without argument, and she could not tell, from her window, whether the bouncer had said anything to them at all.

The faces that were admitted were not the faces of a tavern’s clientele. They were too well-dressed for the lane. They came in fine cloaks and good boots and the careful grooming of people who had washed before going out; they did not come drunk; they did not come noisily. They came alone or in pairs. They came at staggered hours between the ninth bell and the first, with the bulk of them passing through the door between the tenth and the twelfth. They left at staggered hours through the small hours of the morning. Iya did not stay awake for all of the leavings. She slept in shifts on the floor by the shutter, with the stool tipped against the door so that a forced entry would wake her.

The corruption-sense was a constant low pressure at the back of her teeth as long as she sat at the shutter. Different on different patrons. On most it was a faint thickening, the way a smell thickens on a coat that has been hung in a smoky room. On a few it was heavier — denser, more particular. On one woman Iya saw twice across the four nights it was nearly as heavy as it had been on Mirelle. Mirelle herself did not return in the four nights, but Iya recognized perhaps eight of the faces from her first watch by the third, and by the fourth there was no doubt. The Tap was a private house. The same people came and went. Whatever happened inside it happened to a closed circle.

That circle was her wall. She could sit at this shutter for a year and not be invited through that door. She would have to be brought.

She turned her attention, on the third night, to the question of who might bring her.

The women who came to the Tap on the arms of men were the answer. There were several such pairs across the four nights. A heavy-set merchant in a fur collar with a thin nervous girl half his age, a soft-spoken older man with a tall bored woman who looked like she had been to the Tap many times and had stopped finding it interesting, and one man Iya saw twice. Twice in four nights, with two different women on his arm, neither of whom Iya saw enter or leave the Tap on any other night.

He was perhaps thirty-five, well-dressed without being showy, with the broad-shouldered build of a man who had once done physical work and had since taken up easier living. He wore a wine merchant’s badge pinned discreetly inside the collar of his coat where Iya, with her good eyes and the lamp directly above the door, could just make it out. The first night he brought a woman in a plum-colored gown who walked beside him with the careful, charmed deference of a woman who had been told she was lucky to be there. The second night he brought a different woman, in a dress of pale gray, who walked beside him with the same careful deference and the same air of being charmed.

Both women carried the corruption-sense lightly when they went in, the way a person carries the smell of someone else’s smoke. The man himself carried it more heavily, settled, established, a smell that had been on him for years. But he did not carry it like Mirelle. He was not feeding the thing. The thing had set its mark on him long ago and he was simply someone it had finished with, in any meaningful sense; he came to the Tap because he liked to come to the Tap, and he brought women because it pleased him to bring women.

He was, Iya decided on the fourth night, watching him walk back up the lane in the gray light just before dawn, exactly the man she needed.

She set herself to learn his name.

It took her a day and a half. The wine merchants of Brynhollow kept their offices in a clutch of buildings at the edge of the Middle Wards, around a small square called Vintners’ Cross, and a scribe-for-hire who hung about Vintners’ Cross with a writing-board under her arm and a willingness to copy out bills of sale for a copper a sheet was a scribe-for-hire who heard names. By the end of the second day she had three names that fit the build and the badge, and by the end of the third she had narrowed to one.

Corvin Meld. Wine merchant. Late thirties. A house on the upper edge of the Middle Wards. A shop on Vintners’ Cross with a green-painted door. Twice-married, twice-widowed, no children that anyone spoke of. A reputation as a man who paid his debts and did not press for payment of debts owed to him so long as the debtor was pleasant about it. A reputation, also — and this last she had had to listen sideways for, because it was not the sort of thing a man’s colleagues said in the open — as a man with interesting friends and an interesting evening life, which the speakers did not elaborate on and Iya did not ask them to.

On the fourth day she went to his shop and bought a half-bottle of a middle-priced red and exchanged six sentences with the boy behind the counter and learned that the master was usually in on the afternoons of the third and fifth days of the week.

She went home to the cobbler-and-tallow attic and sat on the stool by the shutter and looked down the lane at the Tap’s closed door, and she began to plan what kind of woman would walk into Corvin Meld’s wine shop on the afternoon of the third day and let him notice her.

The third day of the following week was cold and clear, and Iya walked into Corvin Meld’s shop on Vintners’ Cross at a quarter past the second bell of the afternoon, in a plain wool dress the color of weak tea with a fitted bodice and a high collar, and a scarf of cream-colored silk that had cost her nine coppers in a market two streets over and was the most expensive thing she had bought since coming to Brynhollow.

She was not the scribe today. The scribe stayed in a folded bundle in the bottom of her chest at the chandler’s. Today she was Yssa Coldmere — she kept the false name, because false names were easier to remember than several false names — third daughter of a small wine factor in the river-town of Marrow’s Ford, four days upriver, sent by her father to inquire after a particular Vellisian vintage that he had been told a Brynhollow merchant might have in stock. 

The story was a story she had built carefully across two evenings at the chandler’s attic, with a real Vellisian vintage and a real estate that produced it and a real river-town that traded in such things, and she had practiced the small specific phrases of the trade — the ’49 if you have it, the ’51 if you do not, my father will not pay over six silvers the bottle but he is willing at six — until they sat in her mouth like words she had said all her life. 

Her father in this story was a stiff and difficult man who had sent her instead of going himself because he was suffering from a complaint of the joints, and she resented him for it, mildly, in the way of a daughter who has been sent on errands too often. The resentment was the seasoning. A woman who came to a man’s shop with only a story was suspicious. A woman who came with a story and a small private grievance about her father was a daughter.

The bell over the door chimed when she came in. The shop was warm and smelled of cedar and old wine. The boy behind the counter, perhaps fifteen, with a soft chin and the careful manners of a shop-boy who had been smacked enough times to have learned them, looked up from a ledger.

“Mistress.”

“Good afternoon. Is the master in?”

“He’s in the back, mistress, if you’ll wait a moment, I’ll fetch him.”

“Thank you.”

He went through a curtained doorway behind the counter. Iya stood with her gloved hands folded in front of her and looked at the bottles on the shelves with the air of a woman who knew what she was looking at, which she did. The order had taught her wines. The order had taught her many small useful things in the last five years, on the principle that an acolyte who was going to walk into the world’s rooms would need to know how the world’s rooms furnished themselves. 

She had learned wine and horses and the names of cities she had never seen. She had learned to play a kind of card game called vint that the merchant classes played in their parlors. She had learned to write three different hands. She had learned, also, in a small back room of the chapter house with a sister named Verna who had been a tavern-girl before her elevation, how to sit in a chair so that a man would notice her sitting and not notice that she had noticed him noticing. She had not enjoyed that lesson. She had not enjoyed the cards either. She had passed both.

The curtain parted and Corvin Meld came through it.

He was as she had seen him in the lane — broad-shouldered, dark-haired, well-kept. Up close she revised her estimate of his age downward by perhaps two years. He was not handsome in any clean way, but there was a settled good humor in his face that read, to a stranger, as kindness. He came round the counter wiping his hands on a square of dark cloth, smiled at her in the easy professional way of a merchant greeting a new customer, and said, “Mistress. How can I help you?”

The corruption-sense was on him at once. Not heavy. A faint sour familiar smell, settled deep in him, almost domesticated. She kept her face composed.

“Good afternoon, master,” she said. “My father sent me from Marrow’s Ford with a list, but I think the list is half foolish and the rest of it I cannot find, and I have been three days now in a strange city walking from one wine shop to another and being told no, and I am beginning to suspect that my father has sent me looking for bottles that do not exist.” She gave him the small rueful smile she had practiced. “Forgive me. I am not at my best. May I sit?”

“Please. Edrick, fetch the lady the chair from behind the counter, and then go run the order to the brewer’s that I gave you this morning. I’ll be some little while with the lady.”

The boy fetched the chair and went. The bell chimed when he left. The shop, which had not been busy, became quiet in a particular way. Corvin came round to the customer side of the counter and leaned a hip against it and folded his arms loosely and looked at her with the half-smile of a man settling in to be useful.

“Now,” he said. “What’s on the list?”

She gave him the list. The list was real. She talked her way through it, in the half-rueful half-knowledgeable way of a daughter who knew the trade better than her father credited and was not allowed to say so, and Corvin listened, and at two of the items he made small approving noises, and at one — the Vellisian ’49 — he laughed out loud and said, “Mistress, your father is sending you to chase the moon. There are perhaps eight bottles of the ’49 in the world right now and six of them are in a cellar in Vellis I can name to you and the other two are in the cellar of a man in Brynhollow who would not sell them to me for the price of his own house. The ’51 you might find. I have two bottles of the ’51 myself.” He paused. “Three silvers and four coppers the bottle. Which is below what I paid for them. Which I tell you because you will find out, when you go home, that your father knew that already, and I would rather be the man who told you straight.”

“Three silvers and four. My father said six at the most.”

“Then your father is generous and confused, and you should buy both bottles and tell him you found them at five and pocket the difference, and I will say nothing about it.” He smiled. “I have done business with several daughters of several fathers. I find I prefer the daughters.”

She let herself laugh. It was not difficult. Corvin Meld was not unpleasant company; that was the fact of him, and she was honest enough with herself about it. It was the easy company of a man who had decided early in life that being charming cost him nothing and won him much, and who had been right about it for long enough that the charm had set into him like a habit of breath. The corruption-sense sat on him underneath the charm, the way damp sat under the lacquer of a piece of well-kept furniture, and the charm did not know about the damp.

“I’ll take both bottles,” she said. “And I will tell my father five silvers, and you will have made me an accomplice in my first commercial dishonesty, and I will be grateful to you.”

“I am pleased to corrupt you, mistress.”

The word landed in the air between them, very small, and he watched her face for it, and she did not let it land in her face. She let it land slightly lower, in the line of her shoulder, in a small involuntary lift that a man like Corvin Meld would read and would file. She had learned that, too, from Sister Verna. They watch the face. Let the face be quiet. The body says what you mean to say.

He did not pursue it. He was too practiced for that. He moved smoothly back to the bottles and the prices, and they spoke for another quarter of an hour about the trade, and she let him discover that she knew a great deal more about wine than her opening had suggested, which pleased him; and she let him discover that she was lonely in the city, with no one to take her supper with and a difficult father waiting at the other end of a four-day boat trip, which pleased him more.

Eventually, in the way these things eventually went, he said, “Mistress, forgive me, but how long are you in Brynhollow?”

“Another five days, perhaps. Until I have finished my father’s errands.”

“Then will you let me make you a small recommendation. There is a wine bar I keep a friendship with, on Carter’s Row, where they pour a Marrow’s Ford red that you may not have had in some years if you have been at home — they keep a good table and the company is quiet. If you would let me stand you a glass on the evening of the fifth day of the week, I would consider it a courtesy to a fellow trader, and I would promise to bore you only a little about the trade and not at all about my own affairs.”

“That is very kind.”

“It is faintly self-interested. I enjoy company that knows the trade.”

“The fifth day at what hour?”

“Eighth bell. Carter’s Row, the sign of the Black Pear. I’ll meet you at the door.”

“I would be glad of it.”

“Good.” He smiled again, and this time the smile reached down a little further, not into anything coarse, only into a particular attentive interest, the interest of a man marking a thing he wished to come back to. “I’ll have the bottles wrapped for you. Edrick can deliver them to your lodgings, if you’ll give him the address.”

“I’ll come back for them tomorrow. I have other errands today and I would rather not have your boy carrying my father’s wine through the lanes alone.”

“As you like.”

She paid him. He counted out her change. The bell chimed when she left.

She walked three streets in the wrong direction before she let herself stop. She stood under the eaves of a shuttered draper’s shop and put her gloved hand flat against the cold stone of the wall and let her breath out in a long slow line.

She had her invitation. She had her night. She had two days to prepare for it.

She also had — and she made herself name it, walking back toward the chandler’s by the long route, with her face composed and her step unhurried and the cream-colored silk scarf warm at her throat — the small clear knowledge that she had enjoyed herself in that shop. Not for long. Not deeply. But she had enjoyed the particular pleasure of being good at something difficult, of moving a man’s interest exactly where she needed it to go and not a hand’s breadth further, of being seen and not being known. It was a clean pleasure and it was a sinful pleasure, both at once, and she did not yet know which weight to give it.

The sins go, Aldis had said. The knowing stays.

She turned the corner toward the chandler’s and went home to plan what she would wear.

The fifth day came cold and dim, with a low gray sky that promised rain and did not deliver it, and Iya spent the afternoon at the chandler’s attic dressing for it.

The dress had cost her two silvers in a respectable secondhand shop on the lower edge of the High Quarter. Made of dark green wool, fitted close through the bodice, falling clean from the hip, with a modest neckline trimmed in a narrow band of black velvet that had been resewn by a careful hand. It was the dress of a merchant’s daughter who had a little money and not too much of it, which was the woman she needed to be tonight. She had paired it with the cream silk scarf from the wine shop, because Corvin had seen the scarf and would remember it, and a small remembering would set her further into the shape he had already begun to build of her in his mind.

She did not own a glass to dress in front of. She had washed in the basin in cold water and pinned her hair as well as she could by feel, and she had pinched her cheeks twice for color, and she had looked at her hands in the gray afternoon light from the shutter and decided that her hands would do. They were a scribe’s hands, ink at the right thumb she could not quite scrub out, but a merchant’s daughter who did her father’s books would have ink on her thumb too. The cover would carry it.

She did the small private things a woman did to herself before such an evening, none of them holy. She packed a thin purse with enough coin to be respectable and not so much as to be worth robbing for. She tucked into the inner seam of her bodice a folded scrap of waxed paper with three pinches of a particular powder the order made and gave its sisters to carry, which would put a strong man down in a quarter-hour if she could get it into a cup, and which she had been told to use only if the alternative was worse than what came after using it. She did not expect to use it tonight. She wanted it on her body anyway.

She left the chandler’s at the seventh bell.

Carter’s Row was a narrow street of small shops on the line where the Middle Wards began their slow climb toward the High Quarter, lit by hung lanterns and busy in the evening with the comfortable foot-traffic of the kind of people who could afford to take supper out. The Black Pear stood near the upper end of the row, with a black-painted sign and a warm window full of yellow light, and Corvin Meld was waiting for her under its awning when she came up the street.

He had dressed for her. The coat was newer than the one he had worn in the shop, a deep wine-colored wool with a black collar, and his boots had been blacked, and his hair had been combed back and oiled in the way of a man who has decided the evening is worth the small attentions. He saw her coming from a half-street away and his face did the small unguarded thing a face does when it is pleased and the man has not yet remembered to dress the pleasure into something more careful, and then he composed himself and stepped forward.

“Mistress.”

“Master.”

“You came.”

“I said I would.”

“Yes. Forgive me. I have a habit of being surprised when people do what they say they will.” He offered her his arm. She took it. He smelled faintly of cedar from his shop and of a good light cologne over it, and underneath both, the familiar sour smell of the corruption. She did not let her face change.

He took her into the Black Pear. He had a booth held, the kind of small dark booth that an establishment like the Black Pear kept for regulars who had brought company, and he ordered a bottle of the Marrow’s Ford red without consulting her, which she pretended to find charming, and he asked after her day and her father and the bottles, and she answered him in the half-rueful manner she had built for Yssa Coldmere, and she let him talk about himself for a little because men of his kind enjoyed being asked to.

He was not a stupid man. That was the thing she kept having to revise upward. She had cast him in her head, walking down here, as a charming fool, the kind of door that pushed open when a woman leaned against it; but Corvin Meld was not a fool. He had a working mind. He listened. He noticed. When she made a small slip, uttering a phrase about the upriver trade that did not quite fit the cover she had built, his eyes paused on her face for a half-second longer than the conversation required, and then moved on, and she could not tell, after, whether he had filed the slip or only her face. She made herself not slip again.

The wine was good. She drank it slowly. He drank it less slowly and ordered a second bottle when the first was three-quarters gone, and by then he was sitting closer to her in the booth than he had been at the start, and his arm had come to rest along the back of the bench behind her shoulders without quite touching her shoulders, and his voice had taken on the warmer half-pitched register of a man whose evening was going where he had hoped it would go.

When the second bottle was half done he leaned in and said, low, “May I ask you something?”

“Ask.”

“You strike me as a woman who is curious about things.”

“I am my father’s daughter. Curiosity is a trade vice.”

“Then let me ask you. Have you, in your time in Brynhollow, found anything that interests you? Beyond the wine.”

“Such as?”

“The city has a great deal in it, mistress, that the better houses do not advertise. Some of it is foolish. Some of it is dull. A little of it is — “ he chose the word with some care “ — worth knowing about. If you have an evening free, before your father claws you back to Marrow’s Ford, I might show you a corner of the city you would not otherwise see.”

She let the pause sit for the right number of breaths.

“What kind of corner.”

“A private establishment. A friendly one. I keep a small membership there. The company is interesting and the rooms are warm and they pour better wine than the Black Pear. It is a discreet place, you understand. People go there to be themselves and not to be looked at by the people they live next door to. I would say no more about it than that, except that I have brought guests there before, and they have not regretted the evening, and I think you would find it diverting.”

“And what do they do, at this place?”

“They drink. They talk. They keep particular company.” A small, careful, knowing smile. “It is not for everyone, mistress. I would not insult you by suggesting it is. But you strike me, as I say, as a woman who is curious, and I have found in life that the women I most enjoy bringing are the ones who have been told all their lives not to be.”

She let her breath catch — a small thing, well-placed, the kind of catch a respectable woman gives when a respectable man says a slightly improper thing she is not sure she should let pass.

“Master Meld.”

“Forgive me.”

“You should not say such things to a daughter of a respectable house.”

“I should not. And yet..”

“And yet.”

“Will you come?”

She looked down at the table. She turned the stem of her wineglass once, slowly, in her fingers. She let the silence run long enough that he shifted in his seat. Then she said, without quite raising her eyes,

“When?”

“Tonight, if you are willing. The evening is young yet. We are not far.”

“Tonight?”

“I will not press you, mistress.”

“You are pressing me a little, master.”

“A little. Yes.” He smiled. “Forgive me.”

She let herself laugh — small, quick, a little nervous, exactly the laugh of a woman who is letting herself be pressed and is half embarrassed by it. “If you are not far. And if you will not keep me very late. My father expects a letter by the end of the week and I have not begun it.”

“I will not keep you very late. And I will see you to your lodgings myself when you have had enough of the company.”

“Then yes. Tonight.”

“Good.” He laid one hand briefly over hers on the table — warm, dry, settled. The corruption-sense moved against her skin like a draft from an open door, and was gone again when he lifted his hand. He raised his cup. “To the corners of the city, mistress.”

“To the corners of the city.”

She drank. He paid the bill. He helped her into her cloak with a small careful courtesy that would have been touching in another man, and they stepped out together onto Carter’s Row, and he tucked her hand into the crook of his elbow, and they began to walk.

He took her down. Not directly, he took the long way, through the better edges of the Middle Wards first, past lit shop windows and the late foot-traffic of supper-goers, and he kept up an easy pleasant patter as they went, asking her about her childhood in Marrow’s Ford and telling her small unimportant stories about his trade, the kind of patter a man kept up to keep his companion’s nerves quiet. But the route was always trending downward. Iya knew the slope of the city well enough by now to feel it under her boots. Each turn took them a little further from the lamps and a little nearer to the river-smell.

“How much further?”

“Not much. Are you cold?”

“A little.”

He drew her a little closer against his side. The corruption-sense thickened around him as they walked, not from him, she realized, but toward him, as if the closer he came to the Tap the more the thing in it reached out to them in welcome. She made herself walk beside him at the same pace. She made herself not turn her head when they passed the mouth of a lane she had stood at the mouth of, three nights ago, with her hood up and her breath shallow and the demon smell breaking over her like a wave.

They turned into the lane.

The bouncer was at the door. The lamp burned overhead. The sign was a ghost in the wood. Corvin walked her up the lane at the same easy pace, and the bouncer’s eyes moved from him to her and back to him and the bouncer said, quietly, “Master Meld.”

“Brun.”

“A guest tonight?”

“My guest.”

The bouncer looked at Iya. His face did the blank thing his face did. He looked at her for what felt like a long time and was probably the count of three breaths, and Iya let her face be the slightly nervous slightly excited face of Yssa Coldmere being brought to a place she had been told all her life not to go, and after the count of three the bouncer stepped sideways and unfolded one arm and pushed the door open behind him without turning to look at it.

“Enjoy your evening, mistress.”

“Thank you.”

She stepped through. Corvin stepped through behind her. The door closed.

The corruption-sense rose around her like warm water rising in a basin, and she was inside.

The first thing she noticed inside the door was the quiet.

Not silence. The room was full and the room was speaking, low murmurs from the booths, the clink of a cup set down on wood, but it was the quiet of a room where loud was not done. A church-quiet, almost. A library-quiet. The quiet of people who had come somewhere specifically to lower their voices.

The second thing she noticed was the warmth.

Heat hit her face in a soft wall as Corvin closed the door behind them. The Tap was hung with red velvet at every wall and across every booth, and a great fire burned at the far end in a hearth that had been built deeper than such a room needed, and the heat from the fire and the heat from the bodies and the heat from the lamps in their small smoked-glass globes had thickened the air to something almost liquid. She felt it on her face. She felt it under the wool of her dress. The corruption-sense, which had risen around her at the threshold like warm water in a basin, did not subside; it settled, instead, into the warmth, until she could no longer separate the two sensations and was breathing both with every breath.

Corvin let her stand a moment. He had done this before. He knew the threshold needed to be stood at.

“Easy,” he said, low, by her ear. “It’s a great deal at once. Take your moment.”

She took her moment. She let her eyes adjust. The room was perhaps thirty paces long and twenty wide, low-ceilinged, with the heavy beams of an old building that had been built for one purpose and converted for another. The booths along the walls were deep enough to be private and high-backed enough to give the booths beside them no view; in the middle of the room stood half a dozen small round tables in various states of use, with two and three patrons each. A long bar ran along the right-hand wall, polished dark, with a single barkeeper behind it — a thin man of perhaps fifty with a clean apron and the still attentive face of a man who had not been surprised by anything in a long time. There were perhaps thirty people in the room and Iya, in one slow sweep of the eye, recognized seven of them from her four nights at the shutter.

She did not see Mirelle. She had not expected to, tonight; the heavier marked ones, she suspected, would not be in the front room. Of the patrons she could see, the corruption-sense lay on them in the diffuse fog she had felt outside on Corvin — the mark of long use rather than of feeding. This was just the antechamber. The demon was elsewhere.

She let her face do the small overwhelmed thing a respectable young woman’s face would do, on being brought to such a place for the first time.

“It’s — “

“I know.”

“It is not what I expected.”

“What did you expect?”

“Something coarser, I think. I am ashamed to say so.”

“You are not the first to say so. The Tap is not coarse. The Tap is — “ he searched a moment for the word “ — attentive. That is the thing it sells. The world is not, on the whole, an attentive place. People come here to be paid attention to in particular ways, and to pay attention to others in those same ways, and they pay well for the privilege, and the house keeps the doors closed so that the paying does not have to be explained to anyone outside.” He smiled, and it was not an unkind smile. “Come. Let me sit you down.”

He led her to a booth. It was at the back wall, three down from the bar, deep enough that when she slid into it she could not see the door she had come in by — only the long perspective of the room, the bar, the fire at the far end, and the slow settled movement of the patrons at their small attentive business. Corvin slid in beside her rather than across, which she had expected; the booths were built for sitting beside, not across. His thigh did not quite touch hers. The cream silk scarf she had worn for him sat warm against her throat. She was conscious, in a way she had not been conscious in the wine bar an hour ago, of every small surface of her own body.

The barkeeper appeared at the booth’s edge without having been called.

“Master Meld.”

“Vall. A bottle of the hill red. Two cups.”

“At once.”

He went. He returned. He poured. He vanished again, all without speaking another word, and Iya marked it: the Tap’s staff did not chatter. The Tap’s staff did not need to be told twice. The Tap’s staff did the work of the room and absented themselves at every other moment. That was a kind of training one did not get from honest service.

Corvin lifted his cup. “To curiosity, mistress.”

“To curiosity.”

She drank. The wine was very good, better than the Marrow’s Ford red at the Black Pear, better, in fact, than anything she had drunk since leaving the chapter house, where on feast days the order opened bottles its donors had given it and that the sisters drank in small careful mouthfuls and praised lightly. She was careful with it tonight. She took a sip and set the cup down. Corvin took rather more than a sip and refilled his own at once and did not refill hers, which she also marked: a man who refilled his own cup and not his guest’s was a man content to let the guest set her own pace, which was not the behavior of a man trying to pour her into something. Corvin did not need to pour her. The room was doing his pouring for him.

“Tell me about this place,” she said, low. “If you may.”

“I may. Within reason.”

“What is it?”

“It is a club, mistress. A private one. Membership is by introduction only, and the membership is small and stays small by design. The members come for the company of others who share — “ a small considered smile “ — particular tastes. Tastes the city above does not know what to do with, when it knows about them at all, which it generally does not. The Tap exists so that those of us with such tastes do not have to invent excuses, or hide, or put up with the small daily indignities of being misunderstood by our neighbors.”

“What kind of tastes?”

“Various eccentricities. The membership is broad. We do not all share the same particular pleasures. We share only the agreement that pleasure of an unusual kind is a thing worth respecting in oneself and in others, and that there is value in a room where that respect is the rule rather than the exception.”

“That is very diplomatic, master.”

“It is the truth in the form the truth can be spoken in, on a first visit.” He set down his cup. “You will see more of what I mean as the evening goes on. The Tap reveals itself slowly to a guest. That is a courtesy. A first night is not for being told. It is for being shown.”

“And what will I be shown?”

“What the room shows you..”

She let that sit. He was good at this. He had done this before — many times before, she suspected, with many young women who had let themselves be brought down out of the better wards on his arm — and the patter was the polished patter of a man who had learned which words a respectable woman needed to hear in order to not stand up and leave the booth. Respect. Courtesy. Diplomatic. The vocabulary of the careful seduction. None of it was a lie, exactly. It was simply the truth chosen to be said.

She drank a small careful mouthful of her wine. She let her eye travel the room. At the table nearest theirs a man and a woman sat across from one another saying nothing at all; she had her hands folded in her lap and her eyes lowered, and he was watching her face with an attention so unbroken it had the quality of a thing being measured. Two booths down, two women sat on the same side of the bench, one of them with her head leaned a little against the other’s shoulder. Neither of them was speaking. The one being leaned on was stroking the back of her companion’s wrist with one slow thumb, and the companion’s eyes were closed, and there was a small held quality to her face that Iya did not at first know how to read.

The corruption-sense moved through the room in slow, almost peaceful currents. A current would gather, somewhere and would thicken for a long moment, and then would ease, and Iya would feel, in those eased moments, an answering small slackening in the air of the whole room, as if something elsewhere in the building had drawn a breath in pleasure and then let it out.

The demon was being fed. Right now, in this room, it was being fed, in small quiet sips. Whatever was going on in this room was the food.

She did not yet know what was going on in this room.

“Mistress,” Corvin said, and his voice was warmer now, the wine working in him, his arm settling along the back of the bench behind her shoulders without quite touching them. “May I ask. Have you ever been told, in your life, that you should not want a thing, and wanted it?”

“Every woman has been told that, master.”

“Yes. But have you?”

She turned her head toward him. The booth was close. His face was near hers. She could see the small lines at the corners of his eyes, the faint flush the wine had brought up under his skin, the absolute attentiveness with which he was waiting for her answer.

“Yes,” she said, low. “I have.”

“Tell me of one.”

“I will not, master. Not on a first acquaintance.”

“Forgive me.”

“You are forgiven..”

He smiled. He let his arm settle the last small fraction it had been holding back, so that the line of it rested warm against the back of her shoulders through the green wool. He did not press further. He did not need to. The conversation could rest there for a while, and would, and the room would do the rest of his work for him.

She took another small mouthful of the wine, because not-drinking would have shown more than drinking would.

It was as she set the cup down that she became aware, dimly, of the first small physical fact she had been ignoring.

She had drunk three cups of wine across the evening, one in the wine bar, two now, and she had drunk a cup of weak ale at the chandler’s before going out, and she had not eaten since the second bell of the afternoon. Her body was beginning to do its arithmetic. She set it aside, and turned her attention back to Corvin and the warm slow room and the slow currents of the demon’s quiet feeding.
